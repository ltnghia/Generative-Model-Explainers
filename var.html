Classifier-free guidance per scale

Infinity trains with Bitwise Self-Correction. During generation the transformer will sometimes sample a wrong bit. A model trained only on clean targets (teacher forcing) has never seen its own mistakes, so the error stays in the picture. BSC randomly flips bits during training and then recomputes the residual targets for later scales, so later scales learn to cancel earlier errors. Below, the same random bit errors (probability p per bit, at every scale) hit the Infinity-style tokenizer of the current picture.

In the paper, flip probabilities drawn from U[0, p] with p = 30% worked best (FID 3.33, ImageReward 0.775, HPSv2.1 31.05). Flipping bits without re-quantizing the targets gave no benefit, which is what the middle image illustrates: corrupted inputs alone teach the model nothing about how to repair them.

What is VAR?

Large language models write text one token at a time: each new token is predicted from everything before it. Early image generators borrowed this recipe directly. VQGAN, DALL·E 1 and Parti first compress an image into a grid of discrete tokens, then flatten the grid row by row and predict it left-to-right, top-to-bottom, the way you would read a page.

Raster order is a poor fit for pictures. A token near the top cannot see the content below it, nearby pixels in the same column end up far apart in the sequence, and a 1024×1024 image needs thousands of sequential forward passes.

VAR (Visual AutoRegressive modeling, Tian et al., Peking University & ByteDance) keeps the autoregressive recipe but changes what "next" means. Instead of predicting the next token, it predicts the next scale. It first produces a 1×1 token map holding the overall colour and layout, then a 2×2 map, then 3×3, and so on up to 16×16. Each map is conditioned on all the coarser ones, and every token inside a map is predicted in parallel. The paper received the NeurIPS 2024 Best Paper Award.

p(r1, r2, …, rK) = ∏k=1..K p( rk | r1, …, rk−1 ) where rk ∈ [V]hk×wk

A painter works the same way: block in the big shapes, then refine. The visualization above shows this. Drag the Scale slider back to 1 and press Generate to watch the picture sharpen as each scale is added.

Step 1 · Turning an image into multi-scale tokens

VAR has two stages, trained separately. The first is a tokenizer: a VQGAN-style autoencoder whose encoder shrinks a 256×256 image 16× to a feature map f of 16×16 vectors. Its quantizer is replaced by a multi-scale residual quantizer:

  1. Start with the full residual f and an empty reconstruction f̂ = 0.
  2. For each scale k: downsample the residual to hk×wk and replace every vector with its nearest entry in a codebook of V = 4096 vectors. The indices form the token map rk.
  3. Look the codes up, upsample them back to 16×16, pass them through a small convolution φk, add them to f̂, and subtract them from the residual.
  4. The decoder turns the final f̂, the sum of all scales, into pixels.

One codebook is shared by every scale. For 256×256 images VAR uses ten scales, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13, 16, which is 680 tokens in total. Each scale only stores what the coarser scales missed, so the residual's energy falls quickly from step to step. Open the Multi-scale tokenizer tab to watch each stage of one step.

Step 2 · The scale-wise transformer

The second stage is a decoder-only transformer in the GPT-2 mould with adaptive layer norm (AdaLN), as in DiT. For class-conditional ImageNet generation, the class embedding acts both as the start token [s], which predicts the 1×1 map, and as the AdaLN condition in every block.

  • Input for scale k: the reconstruction so far, f̂k−1, interpolated to hk×wk, plus position and level embeddings. Output: a probability distribution over the 4096 codes at each of the hk·wk positions, all at once.
  • Training uses teacher forcing. The whole sequence ([s], r1, …, rK−1) goes through the network in one pass under a block-wise causal mask: a token sees every token of its own scale and of all coarser scales, and nothing finer. Cross-entropy is computed on all 680 targets in parallel.
  • Inference takes K forward passes with a KV cache: sample rk (top-k / top-p sampling plus classifier-free guidance), add its codes to f̂, interpolate, repeat. The decoder runs once at the end.
  • Model family: depth d sets everything else, with width 64d and d attention heads, giving about 73,728·d³ parameters. VAR-d16, d20, d24 and d30 have 310M, 600M, 1.0B and 2.0B parameters.

Why next-scale prediction is fast

For an n×n token grid, a raster model needs n² sequential steps, and with attention over a growing prefix its total compute grows as O(n⁶). VAR needs one step per scale, about O(log n) steps, and O(n⁴) total compute. In practice the paper reports VAR is roughly 20× faster than the VQGAN and ViT-VQGAN autoregressive baselines, and DiT-XL/2 takes about 45× VAR's wall-clock time. The Why it's fast tab races the two orderings with one forward pass per tick.

Results and scaling laws

  • ImageNet 256×256: VAR-d30 improves the autoregressive baseline from FID 18.65 / IS 80.4 to FID 1.73 / IS 350.2 with rejection sampling (1.92 / 323.1 without). This was the first time a GPT-style model beat diffusion transformers on this benchmark.
  • ImageNet 512×512: VAR-d36 reaches FID 2.63, against 3.04 for DiT-XL/2.
  • Scaling laws: test loss follows a power law in model size, for example L ≈ (2.0·N)−0.23 on the last scale, with Pearson correlations near −0.998. That is the kind of predictable scaling seen in language models.
  • Zero-shot editing: if you teacher-force the ground-truth tokens outside a mask and let the model fill in the rest, you get in-painting, out-painting and class-conditional editing without any fine-tuning.
VAR left three obvious gaps: it only handles class labels, a 4096-entry codebook limits how much detail the tokenizer keeps, and the causal attention over all previous scales needs a KV cache that keeps growing. SWITTI addresses the first and third; Infinity addresses the first two.

SWITTItext-to-image

SWITTI (Voronov et al., Yandex Research, CVPR 2025) is a scale-wise transformer for text-to-image generation. It starts from the VAR recipe and makes a series of changes, each motivated by a measurement.

Text conditioning

Prompts are encoded by two text encoders, CLIP ViT-L and OpenCLIP ViT-bigG, whose token embeddings are concatenated along the channel axis. Each transformer block gains a cross-attention layer between self-attention and the feed-forward network. The pooled bigG embedding, plus SDXL-style crop-coordinate embeddings, drives AdaLN, which is where the class embedding used to go.

Keeping training stable

Scaling VAR to text-to-image made activation norms blow up during training. SWITTI fixes this by computing the prediction head in FP32 under mixed precision and by adding sandwich normalization (extra norms after attention and FFN). It also adopts RMSNorm, QK-norm, normalized 2D RoPE and SwiGLU feed-forward layers.

Dropping causal attention

The authors measured where a causal scale-wise model actually looks and found that attention concentrates on the current scale. Earlier scales already reach the model through its input, which is the upsampled running sum f̂k−1. SWITTI therefore restricts self-attention to tokens of the current scale. There is no KV cache, which saves up to 2.3 GB of memory per 1024² image and makes sampling about 21% faster. In human evaluation the non-causal model is on par with or slightly better than the causal variant. Switch the model to SWITTI and open Attention across scales to compare the masks.

Guidance only where it matters

Classifier-free guidance doubles the work at every step. SWITTI applies guidance (scale 6) on the early, structure-defining scales and switches it off on the last two, which are the most expensive. This gives about 32% extra speed-up and fewer fine-grained artifacts, though the paper notes late guidance can still help render small text.

Tokenizer, data and speed

SWITTI reuses VAR's tokenizer with its encoder and codebook frozen, fine-tuning only the decoder at 512² (L1, LPIPS and adversarial losses). The model has about 2.5B parameters (d = 30). It is pre-trained at 256² and 512² with 10 scales, then works at 1024² with 14 scales. Training data is about 100M image–text pairs filtered from roughly 6B web pairs and re-captioned with LLaVA and ShareGPT4V, followed by fine-tuning on about 40K curated pairs. A full 1024² generation takes about 0.5 s on an A100, against 2.3 s for SDXL (25 steps) and 3.9 s for SD3-Medium.

Infinitybitwise tokens

Infinity (Han et al., ByteDance, CVPR 2025) comes from the same group as VAR. Its starting point is the tokenizer: with only 4096 codes, fine detail is lost before the transformer even sees it. Increasing the vocabulary to 2³² codes would make reconstructions much sharper, but a softmax head over 2³² classes would need h·2³² ≈ 8.8 trillion weights at width h = 2048. Infinity's answer is to predict bits instead of indices.

Bitwise multi-scale residual quantizer

The scale-by-scale residual scheme stays the same, but each residual vector is quantized to d binary values, the signs of its d dimensions after projection onto a sphere. This is binary spherical quantization (BSQ). The implicit vocabulary is 2d, and BSQ's entropy term costs O(d) rather than the O(2d) of lookup-free quantization (LFQ). That lets the tokenizer scale to 2⁶⁴ while LFQ runs out of memory at d = 20. The text-to-image model uses d = 32. On ImageNet 256 the 2³² tokenizer reaches rFID 0.61, better than the continuous SD VAE's 0.87.

Infinite-Vocabulary Classifier (IVC)

Instead of one 2d-way softmax, IVC attaches d independent binary classifiers, one per bit. Parameter count drops from h·2d to h·2d, which is 99.95% fewer weights already at d = 16. Bitwise prediction is also more forgiving: two codes that differ in one bit are close in feature space, so a nearly-right guess is still useful. The Prediction head tab shows both kinds of output for any token you hover.

Bitwise Self-Correction (BSC)

Teacher forcing trains each scale on perfect inputs, but at inference the model consumes its own, imperfect samples, and errors pile up. BSC randomly flips bits of each scale with a probability drawn from U[0, p], then recomputes the residual and re-quantizes the targets for all later scales. The model therefore learns to correct mistakes from coarser scales. It adds no extra compute, and p = 30% worked best. Try it in the Bitwise self-correction tab.

Other changes

  • Text encoder: Flan-T5, injected by cross-attention in every block.
  • Positions: 2D RoPE on each scale plus learnable scale embeddings, replacing VAR's absolute position embeddings, which handle varying sequence lengths poorly. This also converges faster.
  • Aspect ratios: a predefined scale schedule for each aspect ratio keeps tokens per scale roughly equal, so Infinity can generate 1:1, 4:3, 16:9 and even 1:3 or 3:1 images. VAR was square-only.
  • Scales: a 1024×1024 image is generated in 13 scales growing from 1×1 to 64×64. Training runs progressively at 256, 512 and then 1024.

Results

Infinity-2B scores GenEval 0.73 (with prompt rewriting), ImageReward 0.962 and HPSv2.1 32.25, against 0.62 / 0.871 / 30.91 for SD3-Medium, and wins 66% of human comparisons with it. It generates a 1024² image in about 0.8 s, 2.6× faster than SD3-Medium. Across model sizes from 125M to 4.7B parameters, validation loss tracks downstream quality closely (correlation −0.983 with GenEval), and larger models benefit more from larger vocabularies.

Where next-scale prediction went next

VAR started a large family of follow-ups. HART pairs discrete scale-wise tokens with a small diffusion model for the continuous residual. FlowAR replaces the per-scale categorical prediction with flow matching, and FlexVAR predicts each scale directly rather than as a residual. ByteDance scaled the bitwise line to Infinity-8B and then to InfinityStar (NeurIPS 2025 Oral), an 8B spacetime-pyramid model that generates 720p video and scores 83.74 on VBench, ahead of HunyuanVideo, while being roughly 10× faster than leading diffusion video models.

About this explainer

The interactive part runs a real residual quantizer on procedurally drawn pictures, so the token maps, residuals and error-correction effects are computed rather than faked. To keep it fast and self-contained, the toy simplifies three things. Its latent space is a downsampled RGB image rather than a learned 16× latent. Its VQ codebook is a fixed 16×16×16 grid of 4096 colour offsets rather than learned vectors (it has no φk convolutions). The Infinity mode uses 12 bits per token, 4 successive binary refinements per colour channel, instead of BSQ's 32 sign bits on a hypersphere. Scale schedules are those of the real models: VAR 256², VAR's 512² schedule as used by SWITTI, and Infinity 1024² at 1:1.
